## 📖 2. The story: how Japanese players went overseas
#### 1. One man and a friendship (1977)
The first was Yasuhiko Okudera. There was no agent. Japan's national team held a camp in Germany, Okudera trained with 1. FC Köln, and the Japan coach was a friend of Köln's coach. That friendship was the whole 'transfer system'. For the next twenty years almost nobody followed.

#### 2. Stars and sponsors (1994–2001)
Kazuyoshi Miura went to Italy in 1994 and Hidetoshi Nakata in 1998. Nakata succeeded, and Italy noticed Japan. Japan's first licensed agents appeared around 1999, and in 2001 Junichi Inamoto joined Arsenal. Many in Europe still asked whether these signings were for football or for selling shirts in Asia.

#### 3. Someone to vouch for them (2003–2010)
Thomas Kroth, a German agent who had once played alongside Okudera, began telling German clubs which Japanese players were worth it. Clubs trusted him because he was one of them. Takahara went to Hamburg, Hasebe to Wolfsburg, where he won the league in 2009. In the Netherlands, a club chairman who did business with Japan asked a Dutch coach in Nagoya for a talent and got Keisuke Honda.

#### 4. The rule that opened the gate (2009)
Until 2009 a Japanese club could demand a fee even after a player's contract had ended. The players' association fought that rule and it was scrapped. Suddenly a player at the end of his contract could walk to Europe for free. Japanese clubs lost money. Japanese players gained freedom.

#### 5. The bargain everyone saw (2010)
Borussia Dortmund paid about €350,000 for Shinji Kagawa. He was one of the best players in Germany within months, and two years later Manchester United paid many times that. After Kagawa, 'Japanese player' meant 'cheap and good' to every sporting director in Europe. Uchida, Okazaki, Nagatomo and Yoshida all moved in these years.

#### 6. Building the bridge on purpose (2017)
A Japanese company bought Sint-Truiden, a club in Belgium, and used it as a first step for Japanese players. Tomiyasu, Endo and Kamada each stayed about a season and moved up. What Venlo had done by accident, Sint-Truiden did as a plan.

#### 7. A normal career path (2020s)
Now more than 100 Japanese players are at European clubs. European scouts watch Japanese under-15s. Some players skip the J.League and go to Europe straight from high school. In 2025 a defender moved directly from Kawasaki to Tottenham for about €5.8 million. In 2026 there were a record ten Japanese players at Premier League clubs. Going abroad is no longer a dream for a few; it is the expected next step.

## 🔍 4. How agents and clubs find players in Japan
- The old way: trips and lists. Kroth watched the J.League in person two or three times a year, kept a list, and met each player's Japanese agent face to face. He also employed a scout in Japan. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/72063?page=4))
- An age filter. He looked at players of about 24 or younger and called 21–22 ideal. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/56686))
- A trusted insider. A coach or former player who knows both countries recommends someone, as the Nagoya coach did with Honda. ([source](https://www.vi.nl/nieuws/hoe-vvv-kennismaakte-met-een-onvergetelijk-japans-fenomeen))
- Player to player. Honda introduced his agent to Yoshida; Yoshida later advised Minamino and Sugawara about Southampton. ([source](https://www.southamptonfc.com/en/news/article/sugawara-inspired-by-legend-yoshida))
- Today: video first. Scouts filter players on data platforms, then travel to confirm. ([source](https://www.hudl.com/blog/japan-pathways-europe-wyscout))
- Younger and younger. European scouts now contact players in the under-15 to under-17 age groups and invite them to academies. ([source](https://note.hudl.jp/n/n49272b21187e?hl=en&gs=8fe2d7305c2e))
- A Japanese agent inside a European agency. The German agency Sports360 has a Japanese agent based in Cologne. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/864629))
- A club with its own Japan network: Sint-Truiden, and Fortuna Düsseldorf's Japan desk. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/270242))
- The league itself. The J.League opened a London office in January 2025 to build contacts with European clubs and support transfers. ([source](https://www.goal.com/jp/%E3%83%8B%E3%83%A5%E3%83%BC%E3%82%B9/jleague-europe-release-20241212/blt96ada6636826d7a8))

#### How big is the business?

#### Why Japanese clubs often got little
- Short contracts. If a contract has ended, no fee is due. Many early stars left for nothing or for small compensation. ([source](https://www.footballchannel.jp/?p=39685))
- Thin contracts. A J.League standard contract is about 3 pages; a Premier League one is roughly 100. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/871477))
- A typical release figure today is roughly ¥100–300 million (ESTIMATE from one agent). He suggests clubs add a share of any later sale. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/871478))
- Money does come back later. Avispa Fukuoka reportedly received over ¥100 million from Tomiyasu's later moves through solidarity payments (ESTIMATE). ([source](https://spaia.jp/column/soccer/ws/19254))
- The J.League earned about ¥4.5 billion from overseas transfers in 2024, which its chairman calls too little. ([source](https://sportiva.shueisha.co.jp/clm/football/jleague_other/2025/09/14/j_14/))

## 🚨 9. Warnings and lessons
- 'Interest' is not an offer. Maya Yoshida says a reported interest often means the player is the third or fourth choice. ([source](https://qoly.jp/q1/17857240))
- Some agents invent interest to keep a player calm when there are no offers, Yoshida warns. ([source](https://qoly.jp/q1/17857240/p2))
- Fraud in lower leagues: agents take a fee and go silent, or deliver a club far below what was promised. Reported most in Germany and Southeast Asia. ([source](https://victorysportsnews.com/articles/8053))
- Players share the risk. The players' union tells players to check an agent's registration, because a player can be punished together with a rule-breaking agent. ([source](https://www.j-pfa.or.jp/intermediary/))
- Abroad is not automatically a step up. Shinji Okazaki says a move from Japan's second division to Germany's fifth tier is a step down, and that promises can break when a sporting director changes. ([source](https://www.synchronous.jp/articles/-/3730))
- Under-18s: international transfers of minors are generally banned. Barcelona was punished and Takefusa Kubo had to return to Japan. ([source](https://news.yahoo.co.jp/expert/articles/5bea8661ec9099616563a465240e6d1f6314d2e2))
- Running down a contract has a cost. Daichi Kamada's exit from Lazio in 2024 became a public row over what his agent asked for. ([source](https://www.footballchannel.jp/?p=583385))
- The ad side is a separate risk. An agent negotiates with clubs; a management office accepts sponsor deals. See the Vera&John deep dive on Maya Yoshida's page.

## ✅ 12. A checklist for reading any player's story (my analysis)
- Who vouched for the player to the first foreign club? An agent, a coach, a chairman, a teammate?
- Was the first club a gateway or a destination?
- Was a fee paid, or did the contract run out? Who decided that?
- Did the agent change as the player rose? Why?
- Who handles the ads, and is that a different company?
- What did the player learn the hard way, and what do they now tell younger players?
