## 📌 1. In short
The legend says: 'the agent who opened Europe for Honda and Yoshida.' The record says something smaller and more interesting. A Dutch club chairman and a former Nagoya coach opened the door. Kiyooka was the players' representative who walked them through it, and for one year he was also the public voice of Japan's players in a pay fight with their own football association.

## 🚪 4. How the Netherlands door really opened
- The chairman came to Japan. Hai Berden, VVV-Venlo's chairman, ran a logistics business with long ties to Japan. He asked for a Japanese player. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/813731))
- A coach made the recommendation. Sef Vergoossen, a Dutchman then coaching Nagoya, proposed Honda. ([source](https://www.vi.nl/nieuws/hoe-vvv-kennismaakte-met-een-onvergetelijk-japans-fenomeen))
- The contract allowed it. Honda's Nagoya contract reportedly had a clause permitting a move if an overseas offer came. Who negotiated that clause is not stated. ([source](https://www.nikkan-gendai.com/articles/view/sports/151218))
- The agent sat on the player's side of the table at the signing. ([source](https://www.elfvoetbal.nl/binnenland/282802/keisuke-honda-d-sensationale-toptransfer-van-vvv-venlo-psv-had-hem-gratis-kunnen-ophalen))
- The same door was used again. VVV's deal for Yoshida was tied to selling Honda. Honda later said he had opened a path at Venlo that Yoshida followed. ([source](https://www.voetbalzone.nl/doc.asp?uid=93652))
- Clients came by referral. On one account, Honda introduced Kiyooka to Yoshida while Yoshida was still in Japan. ([source](https://www.byfarthegreatestteam.com/posts/maya-yoshida-japan-success-story/))
- For the big sale, a local agent stepped in. Honda's move to Russia was negotiated by a Dutch agent. Whether he replaced Kiyooka or worked with him is not stated. ([source](https://www.voetbalzone.nl/nieuws/honda-vertrekt-voor-vier-jaar-naar-cska-moskou/93778))

#### What lasted
- The union itself. Players gained a legal seat at the table, and a standing council with the JFA and J.League was agreed in 2012. ([source](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本プロサッカー選手会))
- The money argument. The union said player payments were only 0.5% to 3.3% of JFA income in 2006–2009. ([source](https://www.j-pfa.or.jp/news/n-00000187/))
- Unfinished business: image rights. Clubs and the league still control them, and Maya Yoshida, union president since 2022, reopened the issue in November 2025. ([source](https://www.soccer-king.jp/news/japan/20251117/2088713.html))
- A link to Yoshida's union work is NOT documented. Kiyooka held a union role in 2010 and Yoshida leads the union now, but no source shows them working on it together. The one documented continuity is the union's 2010 lawyer, Taisuke Matsumoto, who is its executive director today. ## 💡 8. What we can learn (my analysis)
- A gateway is usually a person inside a club, not an agent. Here it was a chairman who liked Japan and a coach who knew the player.
- One success makes the next one easier. The same club took Honda, then Yoshida, then others.
- Agents can change as the career grows. A home-country agent for the first step, a local one for the big sale, family later.
- The rules at home matter as much as talent. The 2009 rule change did more to open Europe to Japanese players than any single deal.
- Be careful with legends. Much of what is repeated about this agent rests on one Wikipedia page.
