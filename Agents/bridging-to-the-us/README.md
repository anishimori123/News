# 🌉 Bridging Japanese athletes to the US (日本人選手とアメリカをつなぐ)

> A beginner's guide to how a Japanese baseball player gets to America, and who looks after daily life once he is there: language, food, housing, training, school and visas. It compares three routes (Japanese pro baseball then the 'posting' sale; a US college; a minor-league contract straight from high school), uses Rintaro Sasaki, Shotaro Morii and Shoki Oda as case studies, lists the companies already doing this as a business, and marks where support is thin for players who are not stars. Soccer (Maya Yoshida) is used only as a comparison.

## 🧒 1. In short
- There are three roads from Japan to Major League Baseball (MLB). The old road goes through Japan's pro league and can take up to nine years. The two new roads skip Japan's pro league. ([source](https://www.mlb.com/ja/news/rintaro-sasaki-announces-plan-to-sign-with-marlins-via-social-media))
- The new roads are faster but poorer at the start. Sasaki took a signing bonus of $210,200 (REPORTED) when a first-round pick in Japan would get about ¥100 million plus bonuses. ([source](https://full-count.jp/2026/07/21/post1993010/))
- Stars who arrive as proven professionals get an interpreter, a trainer and flights home written into their contract, paid by the club. ([source](https://www.si.com/mlb/2024/01/04/yoshinobu-yamamoto-travel-perks-dodgers-contract-mlb-fans-fascinated))
- A teenager in the minor leagues gets free housing and meals at the ballpark, but daily life is still on him. Morii carried a rice cooker and Japanese rice on road trips. ([source](https://www.daily.co.jp/mlb/2026/11/17/0021042437.shtml))
- A small number of companies in Japan now sell this 'bridge' as a service: management and life support, college placement, and agents acting as project managers. ([source](https://www.nikkan-gendai.com/articles/view/sports/382543/3))
- The gap (my analysis): support follows the money. The players who most need help (18 years old, low pay, no English) are the ones with the least help written down.

## 🛣️ 3. The three routes, side by side
All three roads end at the same place. They differ in how long you wait, how much money you get at the start, and who looks after you.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. NPB, then posting | Be drafted by a Japanese club, become a star, then ask the club to 'post' you. #### Rules that opened the new roads
- Until 2020, Japanese clubs had an understanding known as the 'Tazawa rule': a player who skipped the Japanese draft to sign abroad could not join a Japanese club for two years (three if he left from high school) after coming home. It was abolished in September 2020, so going abroad first no longer closes the door at home. ([source](https://www.wbsc.org/news/npb-to-abolish-tazawa-rule-welcome-back-players-who-sign-outside-japan))
- In 2023 NPB changed its rules for players enrolled at schools overseas: a club that drafts one keeps the right to negotiate until the end of the following July. That is how SoftBank could draft Sasaki in October 2025 and wait until after the July 2026 MLB draft. ([source](https://www.hb-nippon.com/articles/3117))
- The posting system itself exists because of three 1990s cases (Hideo Nomo, Hideki Irabu, Alfonso Soriano) handled by the agent Don Nomura. See the Nomo and Nomura pages. ([source](https://anishimori123.github.io/News/sports/baseball/hideo-nomo.html))
- Pay in the minor leagues improved: clubs have had to provide housing since 2022, and the first minor-league labour agreement in 2023 raised minimum salaries (for example Single-A from $11,000 to $26,200 a year). ([source](https://frontofficesports.com/minor-league-players-to-get-big-salary-bump-in-historic-cba))
- Why agents now appear around amateurs: a sports-business professor, Itaru Kobayashi, points out that an MLB first-round pick can earn a bonus above ¥1 billion and even a third-round pick can pass ¥100 million, more than Japan's maximum for a first-round pick. ([source](https://www.nikkan-gendai.com/articles/view/sports/382543))

- Morii (Nov 2026): 「やっぱり白米がないと厳しい」 ("It really is hard without white rice"). The food was better than he feared, but he took a rice cooker and Japanese rice on road trips. ([source](https://www.daily.co.jp/mlb/2026/11/17/0021042437.shtml))

- Shinnosuke Ogasawara on the minor leagues (Dec 2025): cheap connecting flights and buses, not enough food, sharing Japanese snacks with team-mates. 「それを覚悟で挑戦している」 ("I took this on knowing that"). ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/868357))

#### Loneliness
- Morii (May 2026): "So when I feel homesick, I just play baseball." ([source](https://www.foxsports.com/articles/mlb/as-japanese-threeway-prospect-shotaro-morii-finds-his-footing-in-the-minor-leagues))

## 🕳️ 12. Where the gaps are (my analysis)
This whole section is my analysis, built from the facts above. It is not a finding from any one source.

- 1. Support follows the money, not the need. The 27-year-old with $54 million gets an interpreter; the 18-year-old on about $20,000 may not. Yet even the rich star (Imai) said daily life hurt his pitching. The teenager has the same problem with fewer tools.
- 2. Nobody owns 'daily life' for the minor leaguer. The club gives a bed, ballpark meals and coaching. Rice, a kitchen, a phone plan, a bank account, a driving licence, tax forms and a doctor who can explain things in Japanese are nobody's job. Morii solved it with a rice cooker, a room-mate and ChatGPT.
- 3. Visa and paperwork is a single point of failure. Sasaki lost two months; Oda may lose a signing window. I found no company that advertises visa handling for baseball amateurs as its main service.
- 4. The college road has a money hole. A foreign student cannot easily earn sponsor money in the US, a scholarship can be cut, and the draft bonus outside the top rounds is small. A family needs savings or a sponsor in Japan.
- 5. The language work starts too late for most. All three case-study players began years early or chose the road because of language. A normal high-school star who decides in his last summer has months, not years.
- 6. There is no shared map. Each family rebuilds the knowledge from scratch (the Morii family's six-day trip, Sasaki's calls to Ohtani and Kikuchi). The Texas Rangers bringing their own Asian minor leaguers to explain daily life to Oda shows clubs know this is what families want to hear.
- 7. Failure has no service at all. Kinoshita says coming home or transferring should not be called failure, but I found no company whose product is the way back: a Japanese club tryout, a company team, or a university place for a 21-year-old released from Single-A.
- 8. Who pays is hidden. For almost every Japan-side company on this page the fee is 'not stated'. A family cannot compare offers. A clear price list would itself be a product.
- 9. An honest limit. The number of players is small: a handful a year go direct, and about 70 a year go to US colleges in all sports through the largest placement company. A business serving only baseball teenagers would be tiny unless it also served college athletes in other sports or MLB clubs themselves.
