🇩🇪 Thomas Kroth トーマス・クロート

Former Bundesliga midfielder who became the German-side agent behind the first big wave of Japanese players in Germany: Takahara, Hasebe, Kagawa, Uchida and others. German media once called it a monopoly.

📌 Status (2026-11-21): Still active at 67 as head of his agency PRO Profil and as goalkeeper Manuel Neuer's agent (May 2026). No Japanese move to Germany from 2024 to 2026 was found credited to him, so 'still the main route for Japanese players' is not supported.

📌 1. In short

Kroth did not sell Japanese players by flying to Japan with a catalogue. He sold trust. German sporting directors were his old teammates, and they accepted 'Thomas's player' because he only recommended players he was sure about. He usually worked as the German half of a pair, with a Japanese agent keeping the Japan side.

🧠 4. His method: why German clubs trusted 'Thomas's player'

  • He is the German half of a pair. In 2010 most Japanese players in Germany also had a Japanese agent. Only Kagawa and Soma had Kroth alone. [source]
  • He sells a filter, not a player. He says he presents a player only when he is sure the player will succeed, so clubs accept his word. [source]
  • He says selling directly from Japan rarely works. German clubs look first at neighbours like Poland and Croatia, then South America. Japan is far away. [source]
  • His network is his old dressing room. He played with men who became sporting directors and coaches (Zorc, Magath, Allofs). Hasebe said his standing is high, partly because he represents Neuer. [source]
  • He scouts in person. He watches the J.League two or three times a year, has followed it since the mid-1990s, and insists on meeting players and their Japanese agents face to face. [source]
  • He has an age rule. He marks players of about 24 or younger, calls 21–22 ideal, and 28 too late. [source]
  • He uses his German clients as helpers. A German teammate who is also his client looks after the new arrival (Neuer for Uchida at Schalke). [source]
  • He names the hurdles: physique, the German language and food. Kagawa told him food was the hardest. He finds Japanese restaurants and offers support at any hour. [source]
  • He wants ten years or more with a player, from youth to life after football. [source]
  • He warns against over-selling. In 2012 he said not every Japanese talent suits the Bundesliga and hoped the rush would calm down. [source]

Why he thinks Japanese players succeed in Germany

  • Passing quality, work for the team and self-care, even on holiday. His phrase: 「日本人の選手はプロフェッショナルなんだよ。」 (Japanese players are professionals). [source]
  • Motivation, strong will and discipline. He says clubs find they adapt faster than South American players. [source]
  • They do not treat Germany as a stepping stone. [source]
  • The weak point he names: pressing in the J.League is softer and slower than in Europe, which surprises new arrivals. [source]

💶 5. The Kagawa deal (2010): the bargain that changed the market

  • Dortmund paid about €350,000. UEFA described it as compensation, not a transfer fee. Japanese reports call it training compensation of roughly ¥40–48 million. [source]
  • How it started: a scout's tip, an invitation to Germany in December 2009, and a three-year contract agreed in May 2010. [source]
  • Coach Jürgen Klopp said they knew he was good but not that he would adjust so quickly. [source]
  • Two years later Manchester United paid somewhere between €15 million and €22 million for him. [source]
  • Effect: it became the standard example of Japanese clubs letting talent go for almost nothing, and it made every German club look at Japan. [source]
  • Why only compensation was due (contract end or a clause at Cerezo Osaka) is not confirmed in the sources read.

⚠️ 7. Controversies and criticism

No regulatory problem or dispute with a player was found involving him personally (searched 21 Nov 2026; the search was cut short, so this is not a full clearance). The criticism that exists is about low prices for Japanese clubs.

The criticism

  • Unnamed J.League club managers said in 2011 they would stop dealing with him, accusing him of unfair methods to offer their players cheaply in Europe. [source]
  • Kagawa's price: Cerezo Osaka received about ¥40 million for a player later sold for many times more. [source]
  • Okazaki, January 2011: Shimizu S-Pulse got no fee, alleged a double contract and withheld paperwork until FIFA cleared the move. SPOX says Shimizu was angry at Stuttgart and Kroth; a Japanese report names a different agent, Roberto Tsukuda. [source]
  • July 2019: he told a newspaper that Bayern Munich's squad was not yet competitive. His client Neuer publicly distanced himself. [source]

The defence

  • The same SPOX article says the cheap exits happened because J.League clubs offered only one- or two-year contracts. When clubs gave longer deals, they were paid. [source]
  • Japan's own rule change in 2009 let out-of-contract players leave for free. That was a players' rights reform, not an agent's trick. [source]
  • The J.League chairman now describes low transfer income from abroad as the clubs' own business failing. [source]
  • As agents' association president he said clubs should refuse to work with unserious agents. [source]

🔄 8. Is he still the door to Germany?

Probably not in the way he was. The 'monopoly' descriptions date from 2010–2011. Today there are several doors.
  • No Japanese move to Germany from 2024 to 2026 was found credited to him. 'No longer active in Japan' is not confirmed either.
  • A rival German agency, Sports360, now has its own Japanese agent based in Cologne, with clients such as Yukinari Sugawara and Takefusa Kubo. [source]
  • The London agency CAA Base charges Japanese players nothing and listed Yoshida, Muto and Itakura as clients in 2021. [source]
  • Sint-Truiden in Belgium, Japanese-owned since 2017, became a gateway club (Tomiyasu, Endo, Kamada). [source]
  • Of 14 Japanese players in the Bundesliga in 2025/26, only 5 came straight from the J.League; the rest came through Belgium or the German second division. [source]
  • European clubs now scout Japan directly, down to under-15 level. [source]

💡 9. What we can learn (my analysis)

  • Trust travels through people. Kroth's real asset was that buyers believed him. For a player from far away, the question is 'who will vouch for me?'
  • A foreign market needs a local partner. The Japanese agent knows the player; the German agent knows the buyers. Most deals used both.
  • One proof changes everything. Hasebe's title and Kagawa's first season turned 'will a Japanese player cope?' into 'who is the next one?'
  • The seller's contract decides the price. Kagawa was cheap because of his contract situation, not because Dortmund out-negotiated anyone.
  • Gatekeepers do not last. Once the market understands a country, clubs scout it themselves and new agents arrive. Fifteen years after 'the monopoly', there are many doors.
  • Age matters more than players think. His 24-or-younger rule explains why so many Japanese players now leave early.
