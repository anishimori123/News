🧭 How Japanese players get overseas: agents explained

A beginner's guide to the people and rules behind a Japanese footballer's move abroad: what an agent does, who pays, which doors lead to Europe, and what can go wrong.

🔤 1. The basics

WordIn simple words
Agent (代理人)The person who finds clubs for a player and negotiates the contract. Needs a FIFA licence since October 2023. [source]
Management office (所属事務所)A company that handles a player's ads, TV and daily support. Often a different company from the agent. Example: Hidetoshi Nakata's PR company, Sunny Side Up. [source]
Transfer feeMoney the buying club pays the selling club. It is only paid if the player is still under contract. [source]
Zero-yen transfer (0円移籍)A player leaves when the contract ends, so the old club gets no fee. [source]
Training compensation / solidarity paymentSmall payments that go back to the clubs and schools that trained a player. Solidarity is 5% of an international fee, shared across the ages 12 to 23. [source]
Gateway clubA first overseas club that is known for welcoming Japanese players, so the next step up is easier. [source]

🕰️ 2. The history in five stages

StageWhenWhat happened
1. Pioneers, no agents1977–1998Yasuhiko Okudera joined 1. FC Köln in 1977 through a friendship between the Japan coach and Köln's coach. Kazuyoshi Miura went to Genoa in 1994. Hidetoshi Nakata went to Perugia in 1998, managed by a PR company. [source]
2. First licensed Japanese agents1999–2002Nobuaki Tanabe passed the FIFA exam in 1999; his company signed Junichi Inamoto, who joined Arsenal in 2001 as the first Japanese player in the Premier League. [source]
3. The Germany wave2003 onwardGerman agent Thomas Kroth took Takahara to Hamburg, then Hasebe, Kagawa and Uchida. Kagawa cost Dortmund about €350,000 in 2010 and every German club began looking at Japan. [source]
4. Small-country gateways2008 onwardVVV-Venlo in the Netherlands took Honda, Yoshida and Otsu. Sint-Truiden in Belgium, Japanese-owned since 2017, took Tomiyasu, Endo and Kamada. [source]
5. Today: many doors2020sAbout 114 Japanese players were at European clubs at the start of a recent season (an NHK figure). Clubs scout Japan directly, and some players go straight from high school to Europe. [source]
  • A rule change at home made the wave possible: in 2009 Japan stopped clubs charging a fee for players whose contracts had ended. [source]
  • In August 2026 there were ten Japanese players at Premier League clubs, a record. [source]

💴 4. Follow the money

Who pays the agent?

WhereWho paysHow much (reported)
Japan (the old custom)The player5–10% of salary every year. A player on ¥10 million pays about ¥1 million. [source]
GermanyThe club, on the player's behalfAbout 10% of the transfer fee or of a year's salary; some clubs less. [source]
New model in Japan (CAA Base)The clubThe player pays 0%. [source]

How big is the business?

  • OFFICIAL: clubs worldwide paid agents 1.37 billion US dollars in 2025, according to FIFA. [source]
  • OFFICIAL: German clubs paid agents €297 million in 2024/25, according to the German league. [source]
  • REPORTED: in Japan in 2019, players paid agents about ¥1.14 billion and J.League clubs about ¥620 million. [source]

Why Japanese clubs often got little

  • Short contracts. If a contract has ended, no fee is due. Many early stars left for nothing or for small compensation. [source]
  • Thin contracts. A J.League standard contract is about 3 pages; a Premier League one is roughly 100. [source]
  • A typical release figure today is roughly ¥100–300 million (ESTIMATE from one agent). He suggests clubs add a share of any later sale. [source]
  • Money does come back later. Avispa Fukuoka reportedly received over ¥100 million from Tomiyasu's later moves through solidarity payments (ESTIMATE). [source]
  • The J.League earned about ¥4.5 billion from overseas transfers in 2024, which its chairman calls too little. [source]

📜 5. The rules, and how they keep changing

WhenThe rule
Until March 2015FIFA licence by exam. In Japan only licensed agents, lawyers and family could negotiate. [source]
April 2015 – Sept 2023'Intermediaries' (仲介人): no exam, anyone could register with the Japan FA. About 360 were registered in 2021. [source]
From October 2023FIFA licence and exam again. About 4,500 licences at the start; 10,525 by December 2025. [source]
The exam20 multiple-choice questions, 75% to pass. 52% passed the first sitting in April 2023; about 40% passed in 2024. [source]
Fee limits (as written)For the player's agent: 5% of pay up to 200,000 US dollars a year, 3% above. [source]
December 2023After a German court order, FIFA suspended the fee limits worldwide. Licensing stayed. [source]
July 2026The EU's top court ruled. FIFA says its key rules can be justified; lawyers say the court was cautious and national courts still have to decide. FIFA plans talks before a new transfer system on 1 January 2027. [source]

🚨 6. Warnings and lessons

  • 'Interest' is not an offer. Maya Yoshida says a reported interest often means the player is the third or fourth choice. [source]
  • Some agents invent interest to keep a player calm when there are no offers, Yoshida warns. [source]
  • Fraud in lower leagues: agents take a fee and go silent, or deliver a club far below what was promised. Reported most in Germany and Southeast Asia. [source]
  • Players share the risk. The players' union tells players to check an agent's registration, because a player can be punished together with a rule-breaking agent. [source]
  • Abroad is not automatically a step up. Shinji Okazaki says a move from Japan's second division to Germany's fifth tier is a step down, and that promises can break when a sporting director changes. [source]
  • Under-18s: international transfers of minors are generally banned. Barcelona was punished and Takefusa Kubo had to return to Japan. [source]
  • Running down a contract has a cost. Daichi Kamada's exit from Lazio in 2024 became a public row over what his agent asked for. [source]
  • The ad side is a separate risk. An agent negotiates with clubs; a management office accepts sponsor deals. See the Vera&John deep dive on Maya Yoshida's page.

💬 7. What players say

  • Maya Yoshida (Dec 2025): choosing a wife and choosing an agent are the two decisions a player must not get wrong. The final decision on a move should be the player's own. [source]
  • Maya Yoshida (Aug 2026): he nearly moved to China and the Middle East several times, and chose not to. [source]
  • Shinji Okazaki (May 2026): players who expect everything to be arranged for them struggle abroad. [source]
  • Wataru Endo (2024): English clubs now rate Japanese players more highly than before. [source]
  • Agent Nobuaki Tanabe: negotiating takes one or two days a year; the rest of the job is helping the player get better. [source]

✅ 8. A checklist for reading any player's story (my analysis)

  • Who vouched for the player to the first foreign club? An agent, a coach, a chairman, a teammate?
  • Was the first club a gateway or a destination?
  • Was a fee paid, or did the contract run out? Who decided that?
  • Did the agent change as the player rose? Why?
  • Who handles the ads, and is that a different company?
  • What did the player learn the hard way, and what do they now tell younger players?
