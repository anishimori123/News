⚾ Don Nomura 団 野村

The agent who got Hideo Nomo out of Japan in 1995 by finding a gap in the rules, then did it again with Hideki Irabu (1997) and Alfonso Soriano (1998). Those three fights forced Japanese and American baseball to create the posting system. Half American, half Japanese, stepson of the famous manager Katsuya Nomura, he was for years the most disliked man in Japanese baseball offices.

📌 Status (2026-11-21): Still a public figure at 69: his memoir about 34 years as an agent came out in July 2026. His current clients, title and company website could not be confirmed. Recent big Japanese moves to MLB (for example Roki Sasaki) were handled by other agents.

📌 1. In short

Nomura did not win by being friendly with clubs. He won by reading the rulebook more carefully than the people who wrote it, and by being willing to be hated. He could do it because he stood between two worlds: he knew Japanese baseball from the inside (through his stepfather and his own failed playing career) and American baseball law through partners in Los Angeles.

🧠 4. His method

  • Read the rulebook for gaps. For Nomo it was one word: the US–Japan agreement only covered 'active' players, so a retired one was free abroad. [source]
  • Get it confirmed in writing first. The Japanese commissioner's office confirmed the reading by fax on 9 Dec 1994, before Nomo acted. [source]
  • Make the club close the door itself. He had Nomo ask for a multi-year contract he expected to be refused; in his 2026 book he calls it a tricky strategy. [source]
  • Bring American partners. Lawyer Jean Afterman and agent Arn Tellem on Nomo and Irabu; Tellem again on Darvish. [source]
  • Use publicity and the threat of US courts when a Japanese club will not talk. [source]
  • Use the name. He told Sports Illustrated that being a Nomura opened doors in Japan. [source]

In his own words

  • 「交渉で一番大事なもの？ それは誠実さですね」 = 'The most important thing in a negotiation? Sincerity.' (President, 2017) [source]
  • 「2人か3人で臨んだほうが、交渉は有利に運べる」 = 'A negotiation goes better with two or three people on your side.' (President, 2013) [source]
  • On being thrown out of rooms: he says he is rather used to it. (JapanBall interview, 2020) [source]

⚠️ 6. Criticism and the defence

The criticism

  • In 1996 all 12 Japanese clubs were said to have shut him out, and the commissioner would not take his calls. [source]
  • Robert Whiting's 1999 Japanese book about him is subtitled 'the hated agent' (憎まれた代理人). [source]
  • Hate mail called him greedy and a cheater; Yomiuri owner Tsuneo Watanabe called him and Nomo 'bad people'. [source]
  • Irabu case: other MLB clubs were angry that they never got a chance to bid, and his comparison of the San Diego deal to a slave trade drew criticism. [source]
  • Lawsuits from the Hiroshima Carp over Checo ($1 million, defamation). The outcome was not found. [source]
  • Iwakuma 2010: the talks failed, he blamed Oakland in public, and the player then left him. This is the one clear public split with a client that was found. [source]

The defence

  • Everything he did for Nomo was legal and confirmed in advance by the Japanese commissioner's office. [source]
  • Players had almost no rights in 1994: no agents, and many years before free agency. His cases forced the leagues to build a proper route, the posting system. [source]
  • Nomo stood by him: in the first Kintetsu meeting he asked Nomura to come into the room with him. [source]
  • After the Irabu case the American players' union moved to stop players being traded like that without their consent. [source]
  • His publisher credits him with opening the road to the majors for several Japanese players. [source]

🔄 7. Is this still true today?

  • The loophole he used is closed. Since December 1998 the normal route is the posting system or full free agency. [source]
  • Agents are now normal in Japanese baseball, so his old role as the lone outsider no longer exists. [source]
  • His last confirmed headline deal was Darvish in 2012. No current client could be confirmed for 2020 to 2026. [source]
  • He is still heard in public: a 2020 interview, a 2026 memoir, and Japanese newspaper pieces in November 2026 recalling his time as interpreter for pitcher Yutaka Enatsu's 1985 try at the majors. [source]
  • No public comment by him on Ohtani, Sasaki or today's posting rules was found.

💡 9. What we can learn (my analysis)

  • An outsider with inside knowledge is powerful. He knew how Japanese clubs think, but did not depend on them for his living.
  • Rules are written by people, and people leave gaps. He looked for what the text said, not what custom said.
  • Being right is not the same as being liked. He paid for these wins with years of being shut out in Japan.
  • The agent needs the right client. The plan only worked because Nomo was calm under attack and good enough to succeed at once.
