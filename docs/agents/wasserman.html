🏢 Wasserman (now THE·TEAM) ワッサーマン

A Los Angeles sports and entertainment agency founded in 2002. It became the main American agency for Japanese baseball stars (Darvish, Senga, Seiya Suzuki, Yamamoto, Sasaki) through one American agent, Joel Wolfe, building on the door first opened by Arn Tellem and Don Nomura. In 2026 the founder was forced out after his name appeared in the Epstein files, and the company changed its name.

📌 Status (2026-11-21): Renamed THE·TEAM in March 2026 and now fully owned by the investment firm Providence Equity. Founder Casey Wasserman has sold out but is still chairperson of the LA 2028 Olympics. Its baseball agent Joel Wolfe represents most of the top Japanese pitchers in MLB.

📌 1. In short

Your three questions, answered briefly. (1) Japanese agent: I found no named Japanese agent inside the firm. The face is an American, Joel Wolfe; the Japanese side comes from partners in Japan. (2) Business model: it earns a percentage of player contracts and sponsor deals, and it grew by buying other agencies with investor money. (3) LA Olympics: the link is personal, not corporate. Founder Casey Wasserman chairs LA28, but since 2026 he no longer owns the agency. (4) Negotiation: its trademark is the opt-out and other protections for the player, plus a tightly run, equal-rules bidding process. See chapter 6.

🧑‍💼 4. Is a Japanese agent involved?

  • No named Japanese agent inside the firm was found, and no Tokyo office or Japan company (two separate searches, 21 Nov 2026). Every report names Americans: Joel Wolfe in baseball, Jason Ranne and Darren Matsubara in basketball. [source]
  • One Japanese-speaking staff member is named: Mako Allbee, described as from the Wasserman office, interpreted at Yamamoto's Dodgers press conference. Her job title was not found (headline only). [source]
  • Stage 1 (1995–2015): the Japanese side was an outside partner. Don Nomura had the player's trust; Tellem had the American firm. See Don Nomura's page. [source]
  • Stage 2 (today): the Japanese side is a management company in Japan. US media say the high-school pitcher Shoki Oda belongs to 'the same Japanese management company' that helped Yamamoto and Sasaki move. [source]
  • Which company? No on-the-record source names it. One web outlet (Sirabee, April 2024, anonymous source) says it is the advertising giant Dentsu, that Dentsu manages Yamamoto and Sasaki, and that Dentsu preferred this agency for business reasons. Treat this as an unconfirmed claim. [source]
  • No player has said in public who introduced him to Wolfe. A June 2024 US report says Sasaki was already getting guidance from Yamamoto's agent months before he signed. [source]
  • So the pattern is two layers: a Japanese company looks after the player at home (ads, media, daily support) and the American agent negotiates with MLB clubs.

🎯 5. How it wins Japanese players

These are the methods that show up in reports. The grouping is my analysis.
  • A chain of clients. Each star makes the next one easier: Darvish, then Maeda, Senga, Suzuki, Yamamoto, Sasaki. Japanese papers introduce Wolfe by listing his Japanese clients. [source]
  • Being seen in Japan. Wolfe went to Japan with his client Lars Nootbaar on a promotional tour and met people around Yamamoto. [source]
  • Running the process for the player. For Sasaki, about 20 clubs showed interest; meetings were held at the firm's Los Angeles office and limited to two hours each. [source]
  • Working with a partner in Japan instead of competing with it (Nomura then, a management company now). [source]
  • Los Angeles as a base: the office, the Dodgers, and a large Japanese community are all in one city. [source]

Nine techniques that repeat (my analysis)

  • 1. Opt-outs. Almost every deal has an exit so the player can go back to the market if he does better than expected (Yamamoto, Senga, Darvish). [source]
  • 2. Trade health risk for conditions, not for less money. When a club fears an elbow injury, tie the opt-out date or an extra club year to that injury (Yamamoto, Senga). [source]
  • 3. Protection from being moved: a no-trade clause, or 'if you trade me I can leave' when the club refuses one (Suzuki, Senga, Darvish, Yamamoto). [source]
  • 4. Cash early: big signing bonus, low first-year pay. The player gets money early and for certain; the club gets an easier budget (Yamamoto's $50 million bonus). [source]
  • 5. Equal rules for every bidder: same meeting length, same assignment, one neutral place. This creates a contest even when price is fixed, and it is a defence if someone cries foul (Sasaki). [source]
  • 6. Use the calendar. Posting windows force clubs to decide (Darvish 2012 at the final hour; Suzuki after the lockout). [source]
  • 7. Gather several top bids, then let the player pick on fit (Yamamoto). [source]
  • 8. Write daily life into the contract for Japanese players: interpreter, trainer, therapist, flights home, no demotion without consent (Yamamoto, Senga). [source]
  • 9. Speak in public. Wolfe holds on-record briefings, explains the process, and answers accusations directly (Sasaki). [source]

Where even a good agent has little power

  • When rules cap the money. For Sasaki, Wolfe said the differences between clubs' bonus pools were tiny and told him not to choose on them. [source]
  • When only one club is willing. Maeda in 2016: one report says the Dodgers may have been the only club ready to pay the posting fee. [source]
  • When the market is slow. Darvish in 2018 reportedly hoped for $155–175 million and signed for $126 million. [source]
  • Suspicion is a cost too. The Dodgers had kept $2.5 million of bonus money unspent before Sasaki, which fed the talk of a fixed deal. [source]

Who is Joel Wolfe?

  • Title: Executive Vice President and Managing Executive, Baseball. He leads the baseball group together with B.B. Abbott. [source]
  • Background (REPORTED): a stats database lists a Joel Wolfe born 1970, from Los Angeles and UCLA, who played outfield in the minor leagues from 1991 to 1996 without reaching the majors. No article I read ties the two together, so treat it as likely, not confirmed. [source]
  • He learned the Japan side under Arn Tellem, supporting Hideki Matsui, according to Tokyo Sports. [source]
  • His advice to young players: career earnings come from the long run, not the first bonus. [source]
  • He also speaks for the player's situation at home. He said Sasaki was heavily criticised in Japan for leaving young. [source]
  • Controversies about him personally, beyond the Sasaki accusations: none found (searched 21 Nov 2026).

🇯🇵 8. Could a Japanese agency step into this field? (my analysis)

Honest view first: a small Japanese agency is unlikely to beat the American firms head-on at negotiating MLB contracts. But there are real openings, and Japanese companies are already taking some of them. The points below are my analysis, built on the cases on this site.

The entry rules

  • Only an agent certified by the MLB players' union may negotiate an MLB contract. To be certified you apply (fee $2,500), pass a background check and a written exam, and must be named by at least one player. [source]
  • Even recruiting players for a certified agent needs a limited certificate. [source]
  • In Japan, a magazine report notes that only lawyers may act as agents in negotiations with Japanese clubs, so a Japanese agency needs lawyers for the home side. [source]

Nine strategic points

Strategic pointWhyExample on this site
1. Own the home side firstThe player's trust, family, sponsors and media are in Japan. Reported fees are about 20% on sponsor deals against 4–5% on salary, so the home side is also where a Japanese firm can earn most.Dentsu (reported) with Yamamoto and Sasaki; Hakuhodo buying Nomura's agency [source]
2. Partner, but put it in writingA formal co-agent role with an American firm gives the bargaining muscle without building it. The danger is being dropped once the American firm knows the player.Nomura and Tellem on Darvish (2012); the bridge ended when Tellem left [source]
3. Get one certified Japanese agent of your ownOne person with the union certificate changes you from a helper into a negotiator.Yoshinori Hasegawa at Octagon, certified since 2008 [source]
4. Start earlier than the AmericansRelationships formed in high school or early in a Japanese career decide later choices. Players under 25 are capped on money, so fit and trust matter more than price.Sasaki managed 'since high school' (reported); the 2026 race for Shoki Oda [source]
5. Pick a gapPitchers are taken. Hitters, relief pitchers, players coming back to Japan, and foreign players going to Japan are less crowded.Nomura represented foreign players joining Japanese clubs [source]
6. Sell service, not only priceInterpreter, housing, family, training and life after baseball are things a Japanese firm does naturally.Yamamoto's contract writes in an interpreter, trainer and flights [source]
7. Avoid conflicts of interestTaking money from a sponsor while representing the player can destroy trust.The reported 2017 split between Darvish and Nomura [source]
8. Do not depend on one personClients follow people. Train a second agent and sign clients to the firm.Wasserman's own risk if Joel Wolfe ever left [source]
9. Find moneyScale needs capital. Japanese advertising and entertainment companies are the natural backers.Amuse (2019) and Hakuhodo (2025) with Nomura's agency [source]

Who is most likely to succeed?

  • Most likely: a big Japanese advertising or entertainment company that controls the home side and either employs or partners with one certified agent. This is already happening. [source]
  • Possible: a small private agency that picks one gap (for example hitters, or high-school players heading to American colleges) and signs a written partnership with an American firm.
  • Least likely: a small agency trying to negotiate nine-figure MLB contracts alone, without a certified agent or a track record. Players choose proof, and the proof is past deals.

💰 9. Business model

  • Commissions: the agency keeps a percentage of each playing contract it negotiates. Forbes' 2025 ranking (as relayed by The Big Lead) puts its maximum possible commissions at about $956 million, on more than $9 billion of playing contracts and nearly $2 billion of sponsor deals. [source]
  • Rates (REPORTED): a Japanese weekly, quoting an unnamed MLB scout, puts its fee at about 4–5% of salary on team contracts and about 20% on sponsor deals. Not confirmed by the firm. [source]
  • Service: the same report credits its staff's close support for players and their families as a reason Japanese players choose it. [source]
  • Marketing: sponsor and advertising deals for athletes, plus consulting for brands. It calls itself a marketing and talent management company. [source]
  • Growth by buying: more than 20 agencies since 2004, including Arn Tellem's practice (2006), a UK soccer agency (2006), Paradigm's live-music business (2021), the baseball agency Jet Sports (2022), Brillstein talent management (2023), hockey and football agencies (2024–25) and a golf agency (2026). List from Wikipedia. [source]
  • Outside money: Providence Equity has owned most of it since 2022 and all of it since 2026. Investment firms also bought rivals (Goldman Sachs bought Excel). [source]
  • Typical rates in the industry, for scale: 3% on NFL team contracts (the union maximum) and roughly 10–20% on sponsor deals, which unions do not regulate. [source]
  • It is much more than a sports agency now: music booking, brand consulting, talent management, broadcasters, speakers and online creators. [source]

⚠️ 10. The 2026 crisis

Facts as reported. No view is taken here on the people involved.
  • 30 Jan 2026: files released by the US Justice Department included 2003 emails between Casey Wasserman and Ghislaine Maxwell, an associate of Jeffrey Epstein, with suggestive remarks. He said he deeply regrets them, that they came before her crimes were known, and that he had no personal or business relationship with Epstein. [source]
  • He has not been accused of any illegal activity in connection with the documents. [source]
  • Clients left, mostly in music: singer Chappell Roan and about 20 other acts. In sports, soccer star Abby Wambach is the only athlete named as leaving; by May the sports business was reported to be running largely as normal. [source]
  • He stepped back in February, the name changed in March, and he sold his stake (about 40%) in July. [source]
  • Japanese players: no Japanese athlete was found to have left (searched twice, 21 Nov 2026). A Japanese weekly speculated in March that Wolfe might go independent or join a rival; that has not happened as far as reports show. [source]

🏅 11. The LA 2028 Olympics link

  • Casey Wasserman has chaired LA28, the organising committee, since 2017. This is his personal role; the agency does not run the Games. [source]
  • 11 Feb 2026: after a review by an outside law firm, LA28's executive committee kept him as chair. About a week later Los Angeles mayor Karen Bass called for him to resign, as did several county and city officials. In June he said he would not. [source]
  • The International Olympic Committee's coordination chief expressed 'full trust' in LA28's leadership; the IOC president said it would not involve itself in the committee's structure. [source]
  • He was still speaking as LA28 chairperson on 19 Nov 2026. [source]
  • Since the 2026 sale he no longer owns the agency, so the old overlap (one man leading both the Games and an agency full of Olympic athletes) has ended. [source]
  • Baseball is in the 2028 Games: six teams at Dodger Stadium. Japan is expected to qualify through the Premier12 tournament in November 2027. [source]
  • Will MLB stars such as Ohtani and Yamamoto play? Not decided. As of July 2026 the owners support it, but the league and the players' union had no agreement (the dispute includes whether taking part is compulsory, insurance and scheduling). Nothing newer was found. [source]
  • The agency represents Olympic athletes (swimmer Katie Ledecky is one). No contract between the agency and LA28, and no documented conflict of interest, was found. [source]

🔭 12. Can it dominate in the future? (my analysis)

  • For: the client chain in Japanese baseball is hard to copy, and every new star arrives already knowing Wolfe's name.
  • For: an owner that says it will keep funding growth and more purchases. [source]
  • For: scale. It is already No. 2 in the world, and the sports side lost almost no athletes in the 2026 crisis. [source]
  • Against: the business depends on a few people. When Tellem left in 2015 the link to Nomura went with him. If Wolfe left, the Japanese clients could follow him.
  • Against: strong rivals hold the other stars. Ohtani is with CAA's Nez Balelo; Scott Boras and Casey Close also sign Japanese players. [source]
  • Against: the Japanese layer is getting stronger. Dentsu is reported around Yamamoto and Sasaki, and Hakuhodo bought Don Nomura's agency in 2025. If Japanese companies control the player at home, they can choose which American agent gets the job. [source]
  • Against: an investment-firm owner usually wants to sell again in a few years, which can unsettle staff and clients.
  • A limit on who can ever buy it: investment firms that own shares in teams face a conflict if they also own a player agency. That kept several big bidders out in 2026. [source]
  • My reading: strong in Japanese baseball for now, but 'dominate' is too big a word. Power is shifting toward whoever holds the player's trust in Japan.
