🌉 Bridging Japanese athletes to the US 日本人選手とアメリカをつなぐ

A beginner's guide to how a Japanese baseball player gets to America, and who looks after daily life once he is there: language, food, housing, training, school and visas. It compares three routes (Japanese pro baseball then the 'posting' sale; a US college; a minor-league contract straight from high school), uses Rintaro Sasaki, Shotaro Morii and Shoki Oda as case studies, lists the companies already doing this as a business, and marks where support is thin for players who are not stars. Soccer (Maya Yoshida) is used only as a comparison.

📌 Status (2026-11-25): Three young players are testing three different roads at the same time. Rintaro Sasaki (Stanford, then the Miami Marlins in July 2026) has not yet played a professional game because of a visa delay. Shotaro Morii has finished his second minor-league year with the Athletics. Shoki Oda said in October 2026 that he will go to the US and was still drafted first by the Yomiuri Giants. He finished meeting ten MLB clubs on 18 November and met the Giants face to face for the first time on 26 November (Japan time). He has not announced a decision. He cannot sign with an American club before 15 January 2027 at the earliest; MLB's labour agreement ends on 1 December with no new deal yet, and a labour stoppage may delay that.

🧒 1. In short

  • There are three roads from Japan to Major League Baseball (MLB). The old road goes through Japan's pro league and can take up to nine years. The two new roads skip Japan's pro league. [source]
  • The new roads are faster but poorer at the start. Sasaki took a signing bonus of $210,200 (REPORTED) when a first-round pick in Japan would get about ¥100 million plus bonuses. [source]
  • Stars who arrive as proven professionals get an interpreter, a trainer and flights home written into their contract, paid by the club. [source]
  • A teenager in the minor leagues gets free housing and meals at the ballpark, but daily life is still on him. Morii carried a rice cooker and Japanese rice on road trips. [source]
  • A small number of companies in Japan now sell this 'bridge' as a service: management and life support, college placement, and agents acting as project managers. [source]
  • The gap (my analysis): support follows the money. The players who most need help (18 years old, low pay, no English) are the ones with the least help written down.

🔤 2. The basics: words you need

WordIn simple words
NPBNippon Professional Baseball, Japan's top pro league (12 clubs). [source]
Posting system (ポスティング)The way a player under contract in Japan is 'sold' to an MLB club. The Japanese club must agree. The MLB club pays the Japanese club a 'release fee': 20% of the first $25 million of the contract, 17.5% of the next $25 million, and 15% above $50 million. [source]
The 25-and-6 ruleA foreign player who is under 25, or has fewer than six pro seasons, counts as an 'international amateur'. He can only get a minor-league contract, and his signing bonus must fit inside a small yearly budget each club has (the 'bonus pool'). [source]
International amateur signing periodThe window in which MLB clubs may sign amateurs from outside the US, Canada and Puerto Rico. It runs from 15 January to 15 December. The player must be registered with the league office first. [source]
MLB draftEach July, MLB clubs take turns choosing amateur players in the US, including foreign students at US colleges. Each pick in the first ten rounds has a suggested bonus called the 'slot value'. [source]
Minor leaguesThe training leagues under each MLB club. From bottom to top: Rookie, Single-A (1A), High-A, Double-A (2A), Triple-A (3A). Most players spend several years there. [source]
NIL'Name, image and likeness': money a US college athlete can earn from ads and sponsors. Foreign students on a student visa are mostly shut out of it. [source]
F-1 and P-1 visasF-1 is the student visa; it strictly limits paid work. P-1 is the visa professional athletes such as Shohei Ohtani use; the team sponsors it. [source]
Agent (代理人)The person who negotiates the playing contract. For MLB contracts the agent must be certified by the players' union. [source]
Management company (マネジメント会社)A company in Japan that handles a player's media, sponsors and daily support. It is often a different company from the agent. [source]

🛣️ 3. The three routes, side by side

All three roads end at the same place. They differ in how long you wait, how much money you get at the start, and who looks after you.
RouteHow it worksWait and first moneyMain riskWho took it
1. NPB, then postingBe drafted by a Japanese club, become a star, then ask the club to 'post' you. Without the club's permission you wait for overseas free agency.Free agency takes nine years. A first-round pick in Japan gets about ¥100 million plus up to ¥50 million in bonuses (the agreed maximum). Leave before age 25 and you can only sign a minor-league deal.The club can say no. SoftBank has never allowed a posting, which is one reported reason Sasaki did not sign there.Ohtani, Yamamoto, Roki Sasaki, Imai, Murakami, Okamoto [source]
2. US college, then the MLB draftSkip the Japanese draft, enrol at an American university, play college baseball, and enter the MLB draft like an American student.Two to three years. A sports scholarship can cover tuition, housing and food. Draft bonuses fall fast after the top rounds: Sasaki, picked 235th, got $210,200 (REPORTED).You must pass real university classes in English. A foreign student on an F-1 visa can earn little or nothing from sponsors while in the US.Rintaro Sasaki (Stanford), Itsuki Takemoto (Hawaii), Rikuu Nishida (Oregon), Genei Sato (Penn State, from 2027) [source]
3. Minor-league contract from high schoolAsk Japanese clubs not to draft you, register as an international amateur, and sign with an MLB club in the January–December window.No wait to turn pro. Morii's bonus was about $1.5 million (REPORTED by AP; the largest for a Japanese amateur). After that, minor-league pay is roughly $18,000–$44,000 a year (ESTIMATE from 2026 weekly rates).Years in small towns at 18, a new language, and no guarantee of promotion. Earlier players on this road were released after about three years.Shotaro Morii (Athletics), Shoki Oda (planned), earlier Kaito Yuki, Tetsuya Yamaguchi, Junichi Tazawa [source]

Rules that opened the new roads

  • Until 2020, Japanese clubs had an understanding known as the 'Tazawa rule': a player who skipped the Japanese draft to sign abroad could not join a Japanese club for two years (three if he left from high school) after coming home. It was abolished in September 2020, so going abroad first no longer closes the door at home. [source]
  • In 2023 NPB changed its rules for players enrolled at schools overseas: a club that drafts one keeps the right to negotiate until the end of the following July. That is how SoftBank could draft Sasaki in October 2025 and wait until after the July 2026 MLB draft. [source]
  • The posting system itself exists because of three 1990s cases (Hideo Nomo, Hideki Irabu, Alfonso Soriano) handled by the agent Don Nomura. See the Nomo and Nomura pages. [source]
  • Pay in the minor leagues improved: clubs have had to provide housing since 2022, and the first minor-league labour agreement in 2023 raised minimum salaries (for example Single-A from $11,000 to $26,200 a year). [source]
  • Why agents now appear around amateurs: a sports-business professor, Itaru Kobayashi, points out that an MLB first-round pick can earn a bonus above ¥1 billion and even a third-round pick can pass ¥100 million, more than Japan's maximum for a first-round pick. [source]

🎓 4. Case study 1: Rintaro Sasaki (college road)

Born 2005. First baseman. Said to have hit 140 home runs in high school (the count includes practice games). Son of Hiroshi Sasaki, the Hanamaki Higashi High School coach who also coached Shohei Ohtani and Yusei Kikuchi.

💬 8. What players say they struggled with

Short quotes are given in the original language with an English meaning. Longer points are put in my own words.

Language

  • Shoki Oda (Oct 2026): the hardest thing will be 「言葉の壁」 (the language barrier), which is why he wants to go young. [source]
  • Shotaro Morii (spring 2026): "Learning English is so tough." He decided to do all his interviews himself anyway. [source]
  • Morii (Nov 2026): with an interpreter in year 1 he felt distant from the coaches and manager, so he dropped the interpreter. [source]
  • Rintaro Sasaki (Sept 2025): he expected the difficulty of study plus baseball to be a 10. Asked what it really was: 「100です」 ("It's 100"). Some nights he could not sleep at all. [source]
  • Sasaki plans his day in 30-minute blocks from 8 a.m.: weights, classes, team practice, then his own practice. [source]

Food and daily rhythm

  • Morii (Nov 2026): 「やっぱり白米がないと厳しい」 ("It really is hard without white rice"). The food was better than he feared, but he took a rice cooker and Japanese rice on road trips. [source]
  • Tatsuya Imai (April 2026, a proven star with an interpreter and a $54 million contract): he said he had not adjusted to American life, in or out of baseball, and thought it might be one cause of his arm fatigue. In Japan players eat dinner back at the hotel; in the US they eat at the stadium. [source]
  • Shinnosuke Ogasawara on the minor leagues (Dec 2025): cheap connecting flights and buses, not enough food, sharing Japanese snacks with team-mates. 「それを覚悟で挑戦している」 ("I took this on knowing that"). [source]
  • A pitcher who has played in both countries, Peter Lambert, says everything differs: ball, mound, travel, food, rest. [source]

Loneliness

  • Morii (May 2026): "So when I feel homesick, I just play baseball." [source]
  • Hiroyuki Kinoshita, who supports Sasaki (Nov 2026): for a teenager abroad, daily life itself can become the fight before the game starts. He names loneliness and the language wall together, and titles his essay with the wish that the challenge should not be 'a lonely gamble'. [source]
  • Agent Alan Nero: an interpreter alone is not enough; relationships with team-mates matter. [source]
  • Yusei Kikuchi, who nearly went direct from high school in 2009, later said that jumping straight into the minor leagues is hard. [source]

🚨 11. Controversies and criticism

Searched in Japanese and English on 21 November 2026.
  • Who is steering the teenagers? Japanese tabloids and message boards claim an employee of a big advertising agency designed Oda's path, as with Roki Sasaki. No on-the-record source names the company or the person. Treat as unverified. [source]
  • Talent drain. Reporters describe Morii's deal as the start of a 'direct to the majors' era that speeds up the loss of talent from Japan, and a Los Angeles writer said Oda's choice shows Japanese baseball must change how it treats players. [source]

🕳️ 12. Where the gaps are (my analysis)

This whole section is my analysis, built from the facts above. It is not a finding from any one source.
  • 1. Support follows the money, not the need. The 27-year-old with $54 million gets an interpreter; the 18-year-old on about $20,000 may not. Yet even the rich star (Imai) said daily life hurt his pitching. The teenager has the same problem with fewer tools.
  • 2. Nobody owns 'daily life' for the minor leaguer. The club gives a bed, ballpark meals and coaching. Rice, a kitchen, a phone plan, a bank account, a driving licence, tax forms and a doctor who can explain things in Japanese are nobody's job. Morii solved it with a rice cooker, a room-mate and ChatGPT.
  • 3. Visa and paperwork is a single point of failure. Sasaki lost two months; Oda may lose a signing window. I found no company that advertises visa handling for baseball amateurs as its main service.
  • 4. The college road has a money hole. A foreign student cannot easily earn sponsor money in the US, a scholarship can be cut, and the draft bonus outside the top rounds is small. A family needs savings or a sponsor in Japan.
  • 5. The language work starts too late for most. All three case-study players began years early or chose the road because of language. A normal high-school star who decides in his last summer has months, not years.
  • 6. There is no shared map. Each family rebuilds the knowledge from scratch (the Morii family's six-day trip, Sasaki's calls to Ohtani and Kikuchi). The Texas Rangers bringing their own Asian minor leaguers to explain daily life to Oda shows clubs know this is what families want to hear.
  • 7. Failure has no service at all. Kinoshita says coming home or transferring should not be called failure, but I found no company whose product is the way back: a Japanese club tryout, a company team, or a university place for a 21-year-old released from Single-A.
  • 8. Who pays is hidden. For almost every Japan-side company on this page the fee is 'not stated'. A family cannot compare offers. A clear price list would itself be a product.
  • 9. An honest limit. The number of players is small: a handful a year go direct, and about 70 a year go to US colleges in all sports through the largest placement company. A business serving only baseball teenagers would be tiny unless it also served college athletes in other sports or MLB clubs themselves.
