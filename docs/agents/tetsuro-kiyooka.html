🤝 Tetsuro Kiyooka 清岡 哲朗

Japanese football agent who represented Keisuke Honda at his 2008 move to the Netherlands and is credited with Maya Yoshida's move there. Also an executive of the Japanese players' association during its 2010 pay fight with the Japan FA.

📌 1. In short

The legend says: 'the agent who opened Europe for Honda and Yoshida.' The record says something smaller and more interesting. A Dutch club chairman and a former Nagoya coach opened the door. Kiyooka was the players' representative who walked them through it, and for one year he was also the public voice of Japan's players in a pay fight with their own football association.

🚪 4. How the Netherlands door really opened

See also the pages on Hai Berden (the chairman) and Kees Ploegsma Jr. (the Dutch agent).
  • The chairman came to Japan. Hai Berden, VVV-Venlo's chairman, ran a logistics business with long ties to Japan. He asked for a Japanese player. [source]
  • A coach made the recommendation. Sef Vergoossen, a Dutchman then coaching Nagoya, proposed Honda. [source]
  • The contract allowed it. Honda's Nagoya contract reportedly had a clause permitting a move if an overseas offer came. Who negotiated that clause is not stated. [source]
  • The agent sat on the player's side of the table at the signing. [source]
  • The same door was used again. VVV's deal for Yoshida was tied to selling Honda. Honda later said he had opened a path at Venlo that Yoshida followed. [source]
  • Clients came by referral. On one account, Honda introduced Kiyooka to Yoshida while Yoshida was still in Japan. [source]
  • For the big sale, a local agent stepped in. Honda's move to Russia was negotiated by a Dutch agent. Whether he replaced Kiyooka or worked with him is not stated. [source]

✊ 5. The union years: the 2010 pay fight with the Japan FA

This is the best-documented part of his career. In December 2010 Japan's national team players, through their association, asked the Japan Football Association (JFA) for better pay. Kiyooka was the named spokesman.

How the fight went

WhenWhat happened
17–20 Dec 2010The association asks for a win bonus of at least 1 million yen, a share of image-rights income and a retirement fund. A boycott of friendlies is raised. Captain Yuji Nakazawa says a boycott is the last resort. [source]
21 Dec 2010JFA president Junji Ogura dares the players to boycott and says the team will go on with whoever wants to play. [source]
28 Feb 2011The association votes to become a labour union, which gives it a legal right to negotiate. [source]
10 Mar 2011New demands sent: at least 1 million yen per win and half of tournament prize money for the team. The JFA calls this excessive. [source]
11 Mar 2011The Tohoku earthquake. The March friendlies are replaced by a charity match, and the boycott question fades. (That link is an inference; no source says it outright.) [source]
Sept 2011Union status is officially certified. [source]
16 Nov 2012Settlement: the qualifier win bonus rises from 300,000 to 500,000 yen; friendlies become a flat 200,000 yen. Much less than the 1 million asked for. [source]

What lasted

  • The union itself. Players gained a legal seat at the table, and a standing council with the JFA and J.League was agreed in 2012. [source]
  • The money argument. The union said player payments were only 0.5% to 3.3% of JFA income in 2006–2009. [source]
  • Unfinished business: image rights. Clubs and the league still control them, and Maya Yoshida, union president since 2022, reopened the issue in November 2025. [source]
  • A link to Yoshida's union work is NOT documented. Kiyooka held a union role in 2010 and Yoshida leads the union now, but no source shows them working on it together. The one documented continuity is the union's 2010 lawyer, Taisuke Matsumoto, who is its executive director today. [source]

🧩 6. Three Japanese rules that shaped moves abroad

RuleIn simple wordsEffect on going abroad
Transfer coefficient (移籍係数), until Nov 2009A Japanese club could charge a fee even after a player's contract had ended, but only for moves inside Japan.An out-of-contract player was cheaper for a foreign club than for a Japanese one. After the rule ended, clubs without long contracts lost players for nothing ('zero-yen transfers'). [source]
A/B/C contracts, 1999 to 2026A new professional's base salary was capped (about 4.6 million yen on the lowest contract).The J.League itself linked the cap to young players going straight to Europe. Abolished from February 2026, with minimum salaries added. [source]
Image rightsThe standard contract gives clubs the right to use a player's image and requires club permission for media work.Players control less of their own commercial value than in Europe or the USA. [source]

💡 8. What we can learn (my analysis)

  • A gateway is usually a person inside a club, not an agent. Here it was a chairman who liked Japan and a coach who knew the player.
  • One success makes the next one easier. The same club took Honda, then Yoshida, then others.
  • Agents can change as the career grows. A home-country agent for the first step, a local one for the big sale, family later.
  • The rules at home matter as much as talent. The 2009 rule change did more to open Europe to Japanese players than any single deal.
  • Be careful with legends. Much of what is repeated about this agent rests on one Wikipedia page.
