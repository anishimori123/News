# Hideo Nomo (野茂 英雄)

> Japanese pitcher with the twisting 'Tornado' windup. In January 1995, at 26, he 'retired' from the Kintetsu Buffaloes to get out of his Japanese contract, signed with the Los Angeles Dodgers and became the first Japanese player to move to Major League Baseball (MLB) for good. He was called a traitor at home, then won Rookie of the Year. The route was planned with agent Don Nomura. Every Japanese major leaguer since has walked through the door he reopened.

**Last updated:** 2026-11-21  
**📌 Status:** Retired as a player since July 2008. Today (Nov 2026, age 58) he works for the San Diego Padres in the USA: the club's front-office page lists him as 'Special Assistant, Baseball Operations'. In Japan he heads the non-profit NOMO Baseball Club. In November 2026 he was inducted into the Asian Hall of Fame in Los Angeles with its first 'Sports Pioneer Medal'. Where he lives is not confirmed by any reliable source.

## 🧭 1. Going his own way: stubborn, or right?
Short answer: he was stubborn about a very small number of things (his pitching form, how to care for his arm, and where he wanted to play) and flexible about almost everything else, including money and comfort. Each time, he first proved the point with results, and he let other people do the talking. Six patterns show up again and again. The 'honest view' below shows what it cost.

### 🌪️ 1. He never let anyone change the Tornado
From boyhood he twisted his back to the batter to throw harder. Scouts and coaches said it would never work. He kept it, and wherever a coach left it alone he gave everything back.

- According to Robert Whiting, he began twisting as a boy to throw harder and to impress his father. (The 'fifth grade' detail rests on one author.) ([source](https://sabr.org/bioproject/person/hideo-nomo/))
- University and company scouts dismissed him; one line reported in Number: 'With that form, we won't take him.' ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/860808))
- A senior at his company team told him a man may keep one thing he never changes. He decided that for him it was the form. (One article, speaker not named.) ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/860808))
- He signed with Kintetsu on condition that the club would not alter his delivery (Whiting, via SABR). ([source](https://sabr.org/bioproject/person/hideo-nomo/))
- Company-team manager Mitsushige Hamasaki says he saw the value of the twist early and recruited him anyway. ([source](https://gendai.media/articles/-/43540))

### 🩹 2. He treated his arm as something that wears out
In 1990s Japan the common belief was 'throw more to get stronger'. He believed, as American teams already did, that a pitcher's shoulder is a consumable. This was the real root of the clash with manager Keishi Suzuki.

- A beat writer who covered the team says Nomo arrived at 1994 spring camp with shoulder trouble and did not throw in the bullpen at first, while Suzuki demanded 200 to 300 practice pitches. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/861472?page=3))
- 1 July 1994: a complete-game win with 16 walks and 191 pitches. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/865749?page=3))
- His innings fell from 243 in 1993 to 114 in 1994 as the shoulder broke down. ([source](https://sabr.org/bioproject/person/hideo-nomo/))
- Teammates later said the 'Nomo hates practice' image was a misunderstanding caused by poor communication. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/866100))

### 🎯 3. He chose the dream over the money, and took the risk himself
He gave up a salary of about 140 million yen in Japan for a minor-league contract in the USA with no guarantee he would ever pitch in the majors.

- Number's headline calls him the man who gave up 140 million yen and chose a salary of 9.8 million yen. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/860810))
- Dodgers general manager Fred Claire told Nomura a major-league contract had to be earned, so the deal was a minor-league one. ([source](https://www.walteromalley.com/international/courageous-warrior-hideo-nomo-made-the-ultimate-leap-to-change-baseball/))
- In January 1995 Kintetsu offered incentives worth close to 100 million yen to stay. He refused. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/860810))
- Dodgers pitching coach Dave Wallace said he had everything to lose and nothing to gain (via SABR). ([source](https://sabr.org/bioproject/person/hideo-nomo/))

### 🤐 4. He let them talk, and answered on the mound
While Japan called him a traitor, he did not argue back in public. He said almost nothing, went to America and pitched.

- When Nomura worried about the attacks, Nomo shrugged and told him to let people talk (Whiting). ([source](https://robertwhiting.substack.com/p/the-book-of-nomo-chapter-5-the-nomo))
- He later said he worried about daily life without English, but had no anxiety at all about pitching: 「全く不安がなかった」. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/844702))
- Dodgers owner Peter O'Malley said he had the heart of a lion. ([source](https://www.walteromalley.com/international/courageous-warrior-hideo-nomo-made-the-ultimate-leap-to-change-baseball/))
- Manager Tommy Lasorda said he represented himself and Japan with class, dignity and character. ([source](https://japantimes.co.jp/sports/2008/07/19/general/now-retired-nomo-made-huge-impact-on-baseball))

### 🤝 5. He picked people, not just teams
He was loyal to the few people who believed in him and worked hardest for them: manager Ogi, agent Nomura, owner O'Malley.

- At his signing he said the other interested clubs did not have a Peter O'Malley. ([source](https://www.walteromalley.com/international/courageous-warrior-hideo-nomo-made-the-ultimate-leap-to-change-baseball/))
- In the first Kintetsu meeting he insisted that Nomura come into the room with him. ([source](https://bunshun.jp/articles/-/90543))
- A later manager, Phil Garner, noticed that he seemed to do well for people he liked (via SABR). ([source](https://sabr.org/bioproject/person/hideo-nomo/))
- Under Ogi, who left him alone, he led the league in wins and strikeouts four years running. ([source](https://www.baseball-reference.com/register/player.fcgi?id=nomo--001hid))

### 🔁 6. He would not stop until his body made him
After surgery and release he kept trying for three more years: minor leagues, rehab, winter ball in Venezuela, one last month with Kansas City at 39.

- In Venezuela in 2007 he dropped the full windup because it strained his elbow, and kept pitching. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/857554))
- He made the Royals in April 2008 and said he was very happy to pitch in the majors again. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/857553))
- His retirement was announced through Don Nomura on 17 July 2008, with no press conference. ([source](https://archives-new.honoluluadvertiser.com/article/2008/Jul/17/br/hawaii80717061.html))
- Twelve years later he still said that if his body recovered he would want to play again. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/844702))

### ⚖️ The honest view
- He did not leave by a door the league meant to be open. The 'voluntary retirement' route was legal, but many in Japan saw it as getting around the spirit of the rules. Kintetsu lost its best pitcher and received nothing.
- The same stubbornness that people admire also hurt him. He stayed on the mound for 191 pitches out of pride, and his shoulder failed in 1994 and again in 2003.
- Manager Keishi Suzuki deserves fairness. His methods were normal for his generation and had won him 317 games. The reporter who covered the feud now says the press, himself included, built up the 'manager against ace' story, and one teammate says Nomo wanted to keep pitching in the 191-pitch game.
- He was hard on the media. An American writer called him a lousy interview, and Whiting reports he shut out an NHK crew for three years.
- His later numbers are ordinary. After 1997 his ERA was above 4.00 in most seasons, and his MLB career ERA is 4.24. American Hall of Fame voters gave him 1.1%. His importance is what he opened, more than his totals.
- 'First Japanese major leaguer' is wrong. Masanori Murakami was first, in 1964. The accurate claim is: first in 30 years, and first to move for good.
- My analysis: he was right about the things he was stubborn about (the form, arm care, his own ability), and he had expert help exactly where he was weak (rules, law, English). Stubbornness alone would not have got him out of Japan. The plan came from Don Nomura.

## 🌱 6. Roots: where he comes from
- **🏠 A working-class Osaka home:** Born in Osaka in 1968. His father Shizuo is described as a former fisherman who became a postal worker; his mother worked part-time at a supermarket. He started by playing catch with his father at about age five. ([source](https://www.walteromalley.com/international/courageous-warrior-hideo-nomo-made-the-ultimate-leap-to-change-baseball/))
- **🌪️ Where the twist came from:** His father told him to throw with his whole body, like the famous pitcher Minoru Murayama. The twist grew from there during elementary and junior high school. Whiting adds that he wanted to throw harder and impress his father. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/860808))
- **🚪 Turned away by the big schools:** He tried for a strong baseball high school (Kindai Fuzoku is the one named) and was not taken. He went to Seijo Industrial High School, a public school with no baseball fame, where the coaches did not touch his form. ([source](https://www.baseballchannel.jp/npb/134923/4/))
- **💯 A perfect game nobody noticed:** In July 1985, as a second-year student, he pitched a perfect game in the Osaka summer tournament. The papers were busy with the stars of PL Gakuen. He lost 0-1 in the last 16 the next year and never played at Koshien. ([source](https://number.bunshun.jp/articles/-/854435))
- **🏭 The company team that took a chance:** Manager Mitsushige Hamasaki of Shin-Nittetsu Sakai (Nippon Steel's team) had been asked by a Seijo coach to look at the boy. He says he promised to make him a first-round draft pick in three years. ([source](https://gendai.media/articles/-/43540))
- **🥎 Learning the forkball:** He could not get a slider to work, so in his first company year he learned the forkball. Hamasaki says he taught it. Whiting says Nomo slept with a ball taped between two fingers to widen his grip. ([source](https://gendai.media/articles/-/43540))
- **🥈 From outsider to Olympian in one year:** He was not in the first list of 30 candidates for the 1988 Seoul Olympic team. A coach who was there says he made the final 20 by improving through camps and the national company tournament, and that he never complained. ([source](https://www.nikkan-gendai.com/articles/view/sports/276576))

## 🔎 8. Deep dives

### 🕳️ 8.1 The escape, step by step (1994 to 1995)
Short version: Japanese players could not simply move to America. Nomura found one gap in the rules: a player who 'voluntarily retired' was only tied to his old club INSIDE Japan. So the plan was to make Kintetsu itself push Nomo into retirement, by asking for a contract the club was sure to refuse. It worked in under a month.

#### How big was the contract he asked Kintetsu for? Sources disagree
- Whiting: his 1994 salary doubled, on a three-year contract. ([source](https://japanesebaseball.com/writers/display.gsp?id=34924))
- Yahoo Sports: a guaranteed multi-year deal of more than $5 million a season. ([source](https://www.aol.com/star-pitcher-hideo-nomo-agent-195302074.html))
- JapanBall interview with Nomura: six years, $30 million. ([source](https://japanball.com/articles-features/japanese-baseball-historical-profiles/negotiations-nomo-and-nomura-how-one-agent-changed-the-landscape-of-international-baseball/))
- Nomura's 2026 book: a term long enough to cover the roughly five seasons until free agency. No amount in the pages that could be read. ([source](https://bunshun.jp/articles/-/90543))
- My note: all versions agree on the point that matters. It was a demand designed to be refused.

#### Lessons (my analysis)
- Read the actual rule, not what everyone 'knows' the rule is. The whole system rested on one word: 'active' players.
- Get the other side's confirmation in writing BEFORE acting. The 9 December fax made the plan safe.
- Let the other side close the door themselves. Kintetsu handed him the form; he did not have to break a contract.
- The player has to be good enough to make the risk pay. Without the 1995 season this would be remembered as a failure.
